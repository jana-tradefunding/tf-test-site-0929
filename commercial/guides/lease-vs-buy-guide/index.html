Four Ways to Fund Business Equipment

Every equipment finance decision comes down to one of four structures. They differ in who owns the asset, when GST is claimed, and what - if anything - is owed at the end of the term.

Outright purchase means paying cash from working capital or a general business loan. No lender, no security over the asset (nothing for a lender to claim), but it ties up cash that could otherwise fund growth. Chattel mortgage is the most common structure for owner-operators: the lender funds the purchase, you own the asset from settlement, and the loan is secured by a mortgage over the equipment itself rather than a lease. Finance lease flips that: the financier legally owns the asset for the term, you make the repayments, and a residual value (commonly around 10 to 20% of the purchase price) falls due at the end - which you then pay out, refinance, or return. Operating lease is closer to a long-term rental: you never own the asset, repayments are typically lower, and the equipment (and its depreciation risk) sits with the lessor, not your balance sheet.

Tax & PPSR Impact

The structure you choose changes what you can claim, when you can claim it, and what's registered against the asset. None of the below is tax advice - confirm your specific position with your accountant before committing to a structure.

Depreciation & Instant Asset Write-Off
Outright purchase and chattel mortgage bring the asset onto your books immediately, which is what makes it eligible for depreciation claims or an instant asset write-off in the year of purchase. Operating leases and most finance leases don't - the rental itself is the deductible expense instead.
GST Input Credit Timing
Outright purchase and chattel mortgage let you claim the full GST credit on your next BAS after settlement. Finance and operating leases spread the credit across each rental payment over the life of the term instead of one upfront claim.
PPSR Registration
Whichever lender finances the asset - chattel mortgage or finance lease - registers a security interest on the Personal Property Securities Register. This protects the lender's claim over the asset and is a standard, expected step; it doesn't affect your ability to use or operate the equipment.
End-of-Term Residual/Balloon
Finance leases and some chattel mortgages are written with a residual (balloon) amount due at the end of the term. You can pay it out in cash, refinance it into a new facility, or in a finance lease's case, return the asset instead. Factor this into cash flow planning well before the term ends.

Real-World Case Study

NEWCASTLE CIVIL CONTRACTING

A Newcastle-based earthmoving and civil contractor needed a $180,000 excavator to take on a larger council contract. Their broker ran both structures side by side over a 4-year term: a finance lease at 8.4% p.a. with a 10% residual came in at $3,916/month, total cost $206,968 including the $18,000 residual due at the end. A chattel mortgage at 9.8% p.a., fully amortised over the same term, came in at $4,514/month, total cost $216,672 - but with the excavator owned outright and no balloon to refinance. The contractor chose the chattel mortgage: the council contract ran past the 4-year mark, and they didn't want a residual payment landing mid-project. The $9,704 higher total cost was, in their words, "cheaper than the stress of refinancing a balloon while we're still using the machine."

Frequently Asked Questions

What happens to the residual at the end of a finance lease?

You have three common options: pay it out in cash and keep the asset, refinance the residual into a new facility, or return the asset to the financier (less common for equipment already in productive use). Most businesses either pay it out or roll it into a new lease on updated equipment.

Can I claim GST back immediately on a chattel mortgage?

Generally yes - because you take ownership of the asset at settlement, the full GST input credit on the purchase price is typically claimable on your next BAS. This is a general observation, not tax advice; confirm your specific eligibility with your accountant.

Is an operating lease the same as renting?

Functionally, yes - it's structured as a long-term rental. You never own the asset, repayments are usually lower than a finance lease or loan, and the equipment stays off your balance sheet as a lessor asset rather than yours.

Which option is better for tax at end of financial year?

It depends on your position. Outright purchase and chattel mortgage bring forward depreciation and instant asset write-off eligibility into the year of purchase, which can suit a business wanting to reduce taxable income now. Leases spread the deduction across the term instead. Speak to your accountant before deciding based on timing alone.

Can I pay out a finance lease early?

Most lenders allow early payout, calculated as the remaining rentals plus the residual, sometimes with a small early termination adjustment. Terms vary by lender - confirm the payout formula before signing if early exit is a possibility.

What's PPSR and why does it matter here?

The Personal Property Securities Register is where lenders register their security interest over financed assets, including equipment under a chattel mortgage or finance lease. It protects the lender's claim if a business becomes insolvent, and it's a routine, expected part of any secured equipment finance settlement.

Does the calculator on this page account for GST?

No - the calculator above compares pre-GST financing cost only (equipment cost, rate, and term). GST timing differs between structures, as covered in the Tax & PPSR section above, and should be factored in separately with your accountant.

Is a finance lease or chattel mortgage better for a growing fleet?

Businesses adding equipment regularly often prefer finance leases for the lower monthly commitment, refinancing residuals into newer models as the fleet turns over. Businesses planning to hold equipment long-term past the finance term more often choose a chattel mortgage to avoid a residual obligation on an asset they intend to keep indefinitely.
